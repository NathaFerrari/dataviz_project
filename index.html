Well‑Being evolution during crisis

Over the past two decades, Europe has experienced a series of economic and geopolitical shocks that have profoundly shaped citizens’ well‑being. We focus on four crises and visualize how European countries responded through an Aggregate Well‑Being Index.

Introduction to the European context

why this story

To visualise how European countries have responded to these events, we constructed an Aggregate Well‑Being Index combining economic, social and stability dimensions. Scores are weighted based on the association of the indicators with Maslow’s pyramid (basic needs weigh more).

How to read

In the maps: greener = higher well‑being index, redder = lower. Use the time controls in the interactive visualizations to explore trends.

Swiss Stability

The chart illustrates the “Swiss stability pattern”: a long-term trajectory of well-being marked by continuity, resilience, and low volatility, even during major economic or geopolitical shocks. Within the project Well-Being Evolution During Crisis, Switzerland stands out as a benchmark, showing how a country can withstand repeated crises without structural deterioration in citizens’ well-being. Over the past two decades, Europe faced at least four major crises: the 2008 global financial crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the recent geopolitical and energy shock following the war in Ukraine. While many countries experienced sharp drops in economic security, social cohesion, and subjective well-being, Switzerland’s trends remain remarkably smooth, with only moderate and temporary deviations. Economically, indicators like average gross earnings and net disposable income per capita show steady growth. Even in times of uncertainty, no abrupt collapses occur—only mild slowdowns. This is crucial for the Aggregate Well-Being Index, where basic needs and economic security carry higher weights. Income stability helps maintain high overall well-being during crises. The labour market reinforces this stability: employment rates remain high, long-term unemployment is low, and only modest increases in financial difficulties are reported. This contributes to a strong performance in the security and stability dimension, providing a foundation for higher-order well-being outcomes. Beyond economics, social indicators such as perceived safety, social support, and life expectancy remain high and stable. None show dramatic declines during crises, suggesting Switzerland’s social fabric effectively buffers external shocks. This resilience supports sustained well-being across multiple dimensions. Subjective well-being, measured by life satisfaction, also remains high. Small declines during difficult periods are limited and quickly reversed, showing that when basic needs are consistently met, higher-level outcomes like self-fulfilment and life satisfaction are more resistant to crises.
